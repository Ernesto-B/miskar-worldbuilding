### Describing Locations Under Pressure — The Formula

You don't need to be a gifted prose stylist. You need a reliable formula you can execute every time. Here it is:

**One thing they see. One thing they hear. One thing they smell. One thing that feels wrong or interesting.**

That's a complete location description. Four sentences. Done.

_"The Dockyards stretch out in front of you — cranes and masts crowding the skyline, more ships than you've seen in one place. You can hear the groan of rope under tension and men calling out loads and counts. It smells like tar, salt, and something rotten that the tide hasn't carried out yet. A group of dockhands has stopped working and is watching a Valdrath naval officer argue with the harbormaster."_

That last line — the thing that feels wrong or interesting — is your scene hook. It implies something without explaining it. Players will immediately ask about it. You're off and running.

Write this formula on a notecard and keep it at the table. Every new location: see, hear, smell, wrong/interesting. You will never be stuck for a description again.


## CUTTING BETWEEN TRAVEL SCENE AND MONTAGE

The trick is to **close the scene yourself before returning to travel.** Don't wait for a natural ending — create one with a line that simultaneously resolves the moment and implies forward motion.

The formula: **acknowledge what just happened + signal time passing + land at the destination.**

Examples:

_"You leave the refugee family with what food you can spare and push on. The road north gets quieter after that — three more days of farmland slowly giving way to rockier ground. On the morning of the fourth day, the walls of the capital appear over the hill."_

_"The Valdrath patrol lets you pass, but you feel their eyes on your backs for the next mile. After that, nothing but road and the sound of your own footsteps. Two days later, as the sun is setting, you smell the sea before you see it — Aranport is below you."_

The key move is the **time compression phrase** in the middle. "Three more days." "Two days later." "By the following morning." These phrases are a signal to everyone at the table — DM included — that we are skipping ahead. It feels natural because it mirrors how we talk about real journeys. Nobody asks what happened during those three days unless they have a specific reason to.

If a player does ask "what happens during those three days?" you have two options:

- "Nothing eventful — you make camp, keep watch, talk among yourselves. What do you talk about?" (Hands it back to them, generates roleplay)
- Invent one small texture detail: "You pass through a village that's been abandoned recently — fires out, doors open, no signs of violence. Then you move on." (Adds world flavor, implies something without requiring follow-up)


### NPC DIFFERENTIATION WITHOUT VOICE ACTING

You don't need different voices. Professional authors make dozens of characters distinct with zero voice acting. The tools are:

**1. Signature Vocabulary** Each NPC has 2-3 words or phrases they use repeatedly. You don't announce it — you just do it consistently and players' brains pick it up subconsciously.

- **Drevus:** "Reliable." "In my experience." "Naturally." — formal, measured, always implies he's seen more than you
- **Ketta:** "Friends." "Honestly." Finishing sentences with a question — "...doesn't it?" — pulls people into agreement
- **Arveth:** Long pauses before answering. "That's... an interesting framing." Never quite answers directly, responds to questions with adjacent observations

**2. What They Do With Their Hands/Body While Talking** One physical habit per NPC, mentioned occasionally:

- Drevus straightens objects near him while talking — a cup, a candle, a document
- Ketta leans forward when interested, leans back when she's about to say something she wants to sound casual
- Arveth looks at whoever _isn't_ speaking while someone else talks

**3. What They Never Do**

- Drevus never laughs. He smiles, he's amused, but he doesn't laugh.
- Ketta never admits uncertainty — she reframes it as a future opportunity
- Arveth never volunteers information unprompted

**4. Announcing Who's Speaking — That's Fine, Actually** Terry Pratchett wrote footnotes. Stage plays have lighting changes. Film has camera cuts. Announcing who's speaking is just your version of a cut. The issue isn't the announcement — it's making sure something _after_ the announcement feels distinct. Even just a one-word signature before you get into the dialogue helps: _"Ketta — 'Friends, I have to be honest with you...'"_

# Running Combat for Large Parties

## The Core Problem

With 7 players, each turn cycle is roughly 4 minutes. Standard difficulty tools — high AC, prone, disadvantage, status effects that remove actions — punish individual players for up to 8 minutes of sitting and watching. That is not exciting. It is not rewarding. And it does not get harder to solve just by trying harder; it just feels bad.

The solution is not to lower the difficulty. It is to **change the axis on which difficulty operates.**

> **The principle:** Difficulty should come from the *complexity of the situation*, not the *degradation of player capability*. Every player should feel powerful and effective on their turn. The challenge is that the world is demanding enough that even seven capable people have to work together to succeed.

---

## Axis 1: Objectives That Compete With "Just Kill Everything"

The single most effective tool for large-party combat. Add a secondary objective that is **genuinely urgent** — not optional, not "it would be nice if." It must have a concrete, ticking consequence if ignored.

### What makes an objective work

- It requires at least one player to divert attention from the main fight
- Its consequence triggers on a specific round or initiative count (not vaguely "eventually")
- It creates a **real decision** about how to split the party's action economy

### Examples

- A ritual completes on round 4 and summons reinforcements → someone must disrupt it
- A hostage is executed on initiative count 5 of round 3 → someone must reach them first
- A burning section of ship deals +1d6 fire damage per round (stacking) to anyone in the area → someone must put it out or everyone evacuates
- A siege weapon is being loaded and will fire at round 5, targeting a location the party cares about → someone must disable or redirect it

### The "Destiny Raid" Frame

Each player has a natural task that uses their specific strengths. The Paladin protects the hostage. The Sorcerer suppresses the ritual circle with area spells. The Artificer disables the mechanism. The Bard rallies the crew. Nobody is sitting idle. Nobody is doing the same thing. The encounter feels like a team problem, not a damage race.

---

## Axis 2: Positioning Incentives Instead of Helplessness Penalties

Never apply **prone** to a party member through an environmental effect. At a 7-player table, prone costs movement to stand, imposes disadvantage on attacks, and grants advantage to melee attackers — effectively removing meaningful participation for two full cycles (~8 minutes).

Instead, create **positioning incentives** that reward smart play without punishing being in the wrong place.

### The distinction

| Helplessness Penalty | Positioning Incentive |
|---|---|
| Ship lurches → everyone makes DEX save or falls prone | Ship lurches → at start of next round, anyone not holding something is pushed 10ft toward the rail |
| Enemy shoves party member prone | Enemy has resistance while standing in the shadow — players must lure or push it out |
| Earthquake → area becomes difficult terrain and prone | Earthquake → falling rubble targets the two largest clusters of creatures; spreading out matters |

### The "threat to respond to" rule

Environmental effects are more interesting as **threats players can see coming** than as **punishments already applied**.

> **Format:** Describe the threat → give players one round to react → resolve the consequence for those who didn't.

This preserves the environmental tension and challenge while giving players agency. The fear of the consequence is often more engaging than the consequence itself.

### Positioning incentive patterns that work

- **Resistance while in a zone** → must be lured or pushed out (movement/control abilities shine)
- **Bonus damage from flanking** → natural movement without making anyone helpless
- **Targets the closest creature** → melee vs. ranged players have competing goals
- **Half damage from front, full from behind** → creates natural pressure to manoeuvre
- **Cover-granting companion** → kill the companion, expose the boss (creates priority target)

---

## Axis 3: Puzzle Enemies That Demand Coordination

High AC is a trap at large tables. Seven players will still kill a high-AC enemy — it just takes longer, and "longer to kill" is not the same as "interesting." It is mostly more rolls until the same outcome.

Instead, make enemies **specifically vulnerable and specifically dangerous** in ways that require the party to coordinate.

### The pattern

Every puzzle enemy has:
1. **A fiction** — what it looks like and how it behaves in the world
2. **A mechanical pressure** — what it does that demands a response
3. **A key** — the specific action or condition that meaningfully counters it

### Examples

| Fiction | Mechanical Pressure | Key |
|---|---|---|
| Armoured knight with regenerating enchanted plate | Regenerates 10 HP at start of each turn | Cold iron disrupts regeneration for 1 round (History DC 14 to know) |
| Creature that hunts by heat signature | Ignores visual cover, always knows location of warmest target | Obscure heat somehow — or accept it will always find you |
| Shadow wraith that melts into darkness | Gains +4 AC and resistance while in shadow | Force it into torchlight; Sorcerer's light cantrip becomes tactically important |
| Shielded brute with a shield-bearer | Effectively AC 19 while companion lives | Kill the companion first → drops to AC 14 and is now exposed |
| Regenerating horror | Heals through conventional damage | Specific damage type stops it; or seal the ritual powering it |

### Replacing high AC with mobility

High AC models "hard to land a clean hit." The same fiction is more interesting modelled as **mobility**:

- Enemy dashes in, attacks, moves out of melee range every turn → players must think about how to corner or restrain it
- Enemy teleports to a random shadow within 30ft when it takes 15+ damage in one hit → burst damage strategy fails; sustained damage from multiple sources is better
- Enemy climbs/flies and attacks from above → vertical environment suddenly matters; Gunslinger and ranged casters shine

---

## Axis 4: Front-Loading Combat Information

**Any mechanic that determines how a fight can be won must be discoverable within round one, ideally before round one.**

If the party reaches round three swinging ineffectually because they have not cracked the puzzle, that is a design failure, not a player failure. The information must be *available and findable fast*. You do not have to hand it to them — but you must make it findable.

### The four delivery channels

**Channel 1 — Lore before the fight**
A knowledge check before initiative is rolled, or earlier in the session, reveals the key mechanic. Rewards players who engage with the world outside combat and makes Intelligence-based skills feel meaningful.

> *DC 14 Arcana before entering the crypt: the Wraith Knight's armour is a magical construct — it regenerates, but cold iron disrupts the enchantment for a few seconds.*

Relevant skills: History, Arcana, Nature, Religion, Investigation. The check can happen at the Guild board, during a long rest, when examining similar creatures, or when an NPC briefs them.

**Channel 2 — Visual tell at the start of combat**
The mechanic is implied in the opening description if you know what to look for. You describe it in a way that suggests the solution without explaining it.

> *The creature's chest cavity glows with a pulsing orange light. Its hide catches torchlight and looks almost metallic — but the membrane around that glow looks thin, almost translucent.*

You are not explaining the mechanic. You are giving the players a target and letting them discover that hitting it deals more damage. The visual does the work.

**Channel 3 — Enemy behaviour signals it on turn one**
The enemy demonstrates its own mechanic in the first round through its actions.

- An enemy that aggressively repositions out of a flanked position on its very first turn signals "this thing hates being flanked"
- An enemy that visibly regenerates after the first hit signals "killing this normally will not work"
- An enemy that ignores the front rank and goes straight for a back-line target signals "it is choosing targets deliberately — why?"

Players watch behaviour and adapt. This is discovery through observation, not through failure.

**Channel 4 — One free piece of information on a high initiative or Perception roll**
The moment combat starts, anyone who rolls high on initiative or Perception gets one sentence of useful tactical information, framed as instinct or training.

> *Gunslinger, you rolled a 19 — your eye immediately catches that the creature flinches every time the torchlight hits it directly. It has been staying in the shadows deliberately.*

This rewards good rolls with genuinely useful information, not just "you go first." It also seeds the combat with the puzzle without requiring the party to waste rounds figuring it out blind.

---

## Keeping 7 Players Engaged Between Their Turns

With ~4 minutes between turns, players who have nothing to think about will disengage. Here are the structural tools:

**Announce the next two players, not just the next one.** "After Kira, it's Dorin, then Sable." Dorin and Sable are now preparing their turns simultaneously instead of sequentially.

**Ask players to declare intent before their turn.** "Dorin, while Kira finishes — what are you thinking?" They answer while Kira acts. Their turn executes faster because they have already decided.

**Give players things to track.** A visible countdown (ritual completes round 4), a named NPC's HP as described flavour ("the shield-bearer is limping badly"), a secondary objective's status. Players who are tracking something are mentally engaged even off-turn.

**Narrate enemy intent on the enemy's turn.** "The brute turns toward the Paladin — it looks like it is winding up for something big." Now the Paladin is thinking about whether to use a reaction, a defensive spell, or move. They are engaged before their turn arrives.

**Let reactions matter.** Opportunity attacks, Shield spell, Sentinel feat, Cutting Words — any player can be called on to make a meaningful decision during another player's turn. Structure combats so these come up. An enemy that moves through the party's space instead of around it generates more reaction opportunities.

---

## Summary of What Not to Do

| Avoid | Why | Replace With |
|---|---|---|
| High AC as primary difficulty | Long fight with same outcome; more rolls, less interesting | Mobile enemy that must be cornered or restrained |
| Prone from environmental effects | 8 minutes of degraded participation per cycle | Positional threat with one round to respond |
| Blanket disadvantage on attacks | Every player is less effective with nothing to do about it | Specific counter that removes the disadvantage |
| Resistances to everything | Frustrating; party cannot change the outcome | Specific vulnerability that rewards coordination |
| Saving or die effects | Removes a player entirely | Conditions that impair but do not eliminate |
| Combat with one objective (kill it) | No decisions after the first round | At least one secondary objective with a ticking consequence |

# Example Encounter: The Burning Archive

*Context: The party is investigating who burned the Caerath Annex. They track the hired convict to a warehouse in the Dockyards district of Aranport. He is not alone.*

## The Setup

**Location:** A three-storey dockside warehouse. Ground floor has crates and barrels. A rope-and-pulley lift runs to the upper floors. Several oil lanterns hang from the ceiling on hooks.

**Enemies:**
- 1x Varek Sond Enforcer (the convict's handler — came to silence him)
- 3x Dockhand Thugs (hired muscle)
- 1x The Convict himself (trying to escape, not fight)

**The secondary objective:** The convict is trying to reach a skiff tied at the warehouse's river-side door. If he escapes, the evidence of Varek Sond's involvement escapes with him. He flees on initiative count 3 of round 3 if not stopped.

> *Why this objective works: It creates a split decision. Some players need to fight the Enforcer and thugs. Someone needs to cut off the convict's escape route. The Gunslinger is obviously suited to shoot out the skiff's line or wound the convict. The Paladin's speed makes them a good interceptor. But those players are also strong in the main fight — the party has to decide.*

**The environmental element:** The Enforcer kicks over a lantern at the end of round 1. Starting round 2, the fire spreads — 5ft of floor ignites per round. Anyone moving through burning floor takes 1d6 fire damage. The fire will reach the rope-and-pulley lift by round 4, cutting off access to the upper floors.

> *Why this works instead of prone: The fire is a threat players can see coming and respond to. It creates movement pressure — players will naturally shift positions without being forced prone. It also adds a countdown (the lift) that makes the environment feel alive. It does not cancel anyone's turn.*

**The puzzle element (the Enforcer):** The Enforcer wears a Varek Sond officer's insignia that grants him a ward — while he holds the insignia, he has resistance to all damage. He will not drop it voluntarily.

> *Front-loading: Before combat, a DC 12 Arcana or Investigation check while searching the warehouse reveals a wanted notice describing a Varek Sond Enforcer known to carry a warded insignia. Alternatively, the Bard or Wizard can make a DC 13 Arcana check on their first turn to recognise the insignia's function from the faint glow. Alternatively, the first player to hit him notices he takes less damage than expected and can say so aloud — now the party knows and can solve it.*

> *The key: Disarm him (Sleight of Hand or attack the hand), use Telekinesis or Mage Hand to pull it, or grapple and then strip it. The Artificer's steel defender can attempt to grab it. Once it is gone, the Enforcer is a normal fight.*

**Terrain that creates interesting decisions:**
- Crates provide half cover (+2 AC) if you spend 5ft of movement to duck behind one
- The upper floors (reachable by lift or a DC 13 Athletics check to climb the shelving) give the Gunslinger and Sorcerer high ground — +1 to attack rolls from elevation (houserule or just flavour)
- The river-side door is the convict's exit; it is also the only ground-floor exit that avoids the spreading fire

## What Each Player Has to Do

| Player | Natural task | Why it uses their specific strengths |
|---|---|---|
| Paladin | Intercept the convict OR tank the Enforcer | High speed and durability; Sacred Weapon makes them a credible threat to the Enforcer |
| Wizard (Bladesinger) | Reach the Enforcer, attempt to disarm or grapple the insignia | Bladesong mobility gets them into melee; the disarm is a creative use of their unique position |
| Sorcerer | Suppress the thugs with area control; optionally take high ground | Storm Sorcery makes difficult terrain and pushes enemies — perfect crowd control |
| Cleric | Support whoever is fighting the Enforcer; manage the fire if it threatens the lift | Healing word keeps the action economy; fire control uses their divine channel |
| Fighter/Gunslinger | Shoot the skiff's mooring line to delay the convict's escape; pick off thugs | The one player who can reliably hit a specific small target at range under pressure |
| Artificer | Steel defender can grapple or flank; Artificer takes high ground for bonus action healing | Technical solution to the insignia; the defender is a second body in a key location |
| Bard | Cutting Words disrupts the Enforcer when he hits; Inspiration where needed; potentially Charm the convict | The convict is not a fighter — a Charisma-based approach stops the chase nonviolently |

> *Notice that the Bard has a completely non-violent solution path to the secondary objective. A charmed or persuaded convict who agrees to testify is more valuable than a dead or escaped one. If the Bard goes that route, the party potentially gets better information than if they just caught him.*

## What You Would Change For Different Parties or Situations

**If the party is too powerful for this level:** Add a fourth thug who has already set a second fire at round 0; the fire clock is faster. Or the Enforcer has a backup insignia — they have to strip two items.

**If the party is struggling:** The convict is not actually loyal to Varek Sond; he is terrified. A DC 10 Insight check on his behaviour reveals he is looking for an out. Any player who calls out "we're not here to kill you, we need what you know" (no roll required, just the words) stops his escape attempt and he surrenders if the Enforcer is dealt with.

**If you want more verticality:** The Enforcer is on the upper floor at start of combat, throwing crates down. Reaching him requires the lift (which the thugs are guarding) or the climb. Now the terrain is a dungeon-style problem.

**If you want a completely non-combat solution:** The party could have found this warehouse address earlier and set up an ambush. Or they could arrive before the Enforcer does, intercept the convict alone, and avoid the fight entirely. The encounter is designed to be fightable — but the best outcome (convict alive, Enforcer neutralised or fled, evidence secured) is achievable through multiple routes.

# Encounter Design Template & Step-by-Step Guide

*Use this for every combat encounter you design, regardless of campaign chapter.*

---

## Step 1 — Define the Fiction First

Before any mechanics, answer in one sentence: **what is actually happening here, and why?**

> *"The party has cornered a Varek Sond agent who is trying to destroy evidence while his hired muscle buys him time."*

Everything that follows should feel like a natural extension of this sentence. If a mechanic does not make sense given the fiction, cut it.

---

## Step 2 — Define the Win Condition and the Interesting Failure

**Win condition:** What does full success look like? (Not just "enemies dead" — what is the best possible outcome?)

**Interesting failure:** What happens if they lose or partially succeed? Is it a dead end, or does it open a different path? Failure should create consequences, not just a game over.

> *Win: Convict captured alive, Enforcer fled or arrested, fire contained, evidence recovered.*
> *Interesting failure: Convict escapes but drops a document. They now have partial evidence — enough to suspect Varek Sond but not enough to prove it. The next session becomes about finding corroboration.*

---

## Step 3 — Design the Secondary Objective

Every encounter should have at least one secondary objective. It must be:

- [ ] **Urgent** — has a specific round or initiative count when it resolves
- [ ] **Meaningful** — the party cares about the consequence (not just XP)
- [ ] **Solvable without stopping the main fight** — at least one party member can address it while others continue

Write the countdown explicitly: *"The convict flees on initiative 3 of round 3 if not stopped."*

---

## Step 4 — Design the Environment Using the Three-Layer System

**Layer 1 — Terrain that creates decisions (not penalties)**
What can be used, climbed, hidden behind, destroyed, or leveraged? At least two options.

**Layer 2 — Environmental threat with one round of warning**
What changes during the fight that the party must respond to? When does it trigger? What is the consequence for ignoring it?

> *Format: "At the end of round [X], [thing happens]. Starting round [X+1], [ongoing consequence]."*

**Layer 3 — One feature that specifically benefits a party member who is not a primary damage dealer**
Bard, Artificer, Cleric, or whoever tends to have fewer "cool moments" in combat — give them an environmental tool that is theirs specifically.

---

## Step 5 — Design the Puzzle Enemy (if applicable)

Not every encounter needs one. Use for boss fights, elite enemies, and encounters where you want the party to think rather than just swing.

Fill in this pattern:

| Field | Your answer |
|---|---|
| **Fiction** | What does this enemy look like and how does it behave? |
| **Mechanical pressure** | What does it do that demands a response? |
| **The key** | What specific action or condition counters it? |
| **How players discover the key** | Which front-loading channel? (Lore / Visual / Behaviour / Initiative roll) |
| **What skill unlocks the lore** | History / Arcana / Nature / Religion / Investigation — DC ___ |

---

## Step 6 — Front-Load the Key Mechanic

For every puzzle element you designed in Step 5, choose at least **two** of the four delivery channels:

- [ ] **Lore** — what skill check, when, what DC, what information is revealed?
- [ ] **Visual tell** — write the two-sentence description that implies the solution
- [ ] **Behaviour signal** — what does the enemy do on its first turn that shows the player how it works?
- [ ] **Initiative/Perception reward** — what one sentence does a high roller learn?

If you cannot fill in at least two of these, the mechanic is not ready. Either simplify it or add more front-loading.

---

## Step 7 — Assign Natural Tasks Per Player

Go through your party roster. For each player, answer: **what does this encounter give them specifically to do?**

| Player / Class | Natural task in this encounter | Which ability/feature does it use? |
|---|---|---|
| Paladin | | |
| Wizard (Bladesinger) | | |
| Sorcerer (Storm) | | |
| Cleric (Eldritch) | | |
| Fighter / Gunslinger | | |
| Artificer (Battle Smith) | | |
| Bard (College of Creation) | | |

If any player has no clear task, either add an element that creates one or adjust the encounter. A player with no natural task will default to "I attack the nearest enemy" every turn.

---

## Step 8 — Check Against the "What Not to Do" List

Before finalising, run through this checklist:

- [ ] Is the primary difficulty coming from situation complexity rather than player capability degradation?
- [ ] Are there any effects that apply prone to players through environmental events? *(Replace with positional threat + one round of warning)*
- [ ] Is there any enemy with AC 18+ whose only function is to be hard to hit? *(Replace with mobility or puzzle mechanic)*
- [ ] Does every player have something meaningful to do on every turn? *(If not, add a secondary objective or terrain feature)*
- [ ] Is the key mechanic discoverable within round one? *(If not, add a front-loading channel)*
- [ ] Is there at least one non-violent solution path, even a partial one? *(Especially for social/investigation encounters that escalate into combat)*

---

## Step 9 — Write Your Combat Notes

Use this format for your session reference sheet:

```
ENCOUNTER: [Name]
Location: [Setting — 2 sentences max]
Fiction: [What is actually happening and why]

ENEMIES
- [Name]: [One sentence on their role. Key mechanic if applicable.]
- [Name]: [One sentence on their role.]

SECONDARY OBJECTIVE
[What it is] → resolves round [X], initiative [X]
Consequence if ignored: [One sentence]

ENVIRONMENTAL THREAT
Triggers: end of round [X]
Ongoing: [One sentence on what changes each round after]

KEY MECHANIC FRONT-LOADING
□ Lore: DC [X] [Skill] reveals [information]
□ Visual: "[Two-sentence opening description that implies the solution]"
□ Behaviour: [What the enemy does on turn 1 that signals the mechanic]
□ Initiative reward: "[One sentence for a player who rolls 17+]"

WIN / INTERESTING FAIL
Win: [Best possible outcome]
Fail: [What opens up, not what closes down]
```

## Quick Reference Card

*Keep this on your DM screen or reference sheet.*

**The Core Shift:** Complexity of situation > Degradation of capability

**Secondary objective must be:** Urgent (specific round) · Meaningful (party cares) · Parallel (doesn't stop the main fight)

**Environmental threats:** Threat with one round warning > Punishment already applied

**Puzzle enemy:** Fiction → Pressure → Key → Front-load via 2 channels

**Front-load channels:** Lore check · Visual tell · Behaviour signal · Initiative reward

**Between-turn engagement:** Announce next two players · Ask intent early · Give things to track · Narrate enemy intent · Make reactions matter

**The test:** Can every player answer "what am I trying to do this turn" the moment their turn starts? If yes, the encounter is working.

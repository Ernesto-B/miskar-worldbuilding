The reason this feels hard is that you're thinking about it wrong. You don't need to invent quests. You need a **menu of quest skeletons** that you can dress with whatever nouns are currently in your world.

A quest skeleton is a situation type with the specifics left blank. You fill in the blanks with whatever's already on your reference sheet — a faction, an NPC, a landmark, a threat you've already established.

Here are twelve skeletons that cover almost everything a Guild board would plausibly offer. Memorize the shape, not the content:

- **Retrieval:** Someone lost or left something somewhere dangerous. Get it back.
- **Escort:** Someone needs to get from A to B. Things will try to stop them.
- **Extermination:** Something is in a place it shouldn't be. Remove it.
- **Investigation:** Something happened and nobody knows why. Find out.
- **Protection:** Something is being targeted. Keep it intact until the threat passes.
- **Delivery:** Something needs to reach someone without being intercepted.
- **Negotiation:** Two parties won't talk to each other. Serve as the bridge.
- **Sabotage:** Something is working. Make it stop working.
- **Extraction:** Someone is somewhere they shouldn't be. Get them out.
- **Surveillance:** Watch something or someone and report back.
- **Competition:** Someone else is also trying to accomplish this. Beat them to it.
- **Containment:** Something is loose or spreading. Stop it before it reaches X.

Now watch how fast you can build a quest at the table. Party walks to the Guild board. You pick a skeleton — say, Retrieval. You grab two nouns from your reference sheet: the Harrow Crane (landmark) and Varek Sond (faction). You now have:

_"A Varek Sond shipping crate was unloaded from the wrong vessel at the Harrow Crane two days ago. The contents are listed as 'surveying equipment.' The merchant who owns it is offering 40 gold to whoever brings it to him before the city watch impounds it tomorrow morning — apparently the paperwork is irregular enough that he'd rather not have officials opening it."_

That took thirty seconds. It's a Retrieval skeleton wearing Aranport clothes. The party doesn't know that. They just see a job that implies something suspicious about Varek Sond, involves a location they already know, and has a time pressure element. They'll bite.

The key move is **always adding one implication that connects to something already in play.** A quest that's purely self-contained is fine but forgettable. A quest that makes the party think "wait, does this connect to the library burning?" is memorable even if it doesn't actually connect — the suspicion itself does the work.

### The Faction Filter

Every Guild quest in Aranport should feel like it could have been posted by one of three types of clients: someone affiliated with a faction, a neutral civilian, or someone trying to stay anonymous. The faction affiliation changes the subtext without changing the skeleton.

Same Retrieval quest posted by a Rhiwmor-affiliated merchant instead of Varek Sond: now the party wonders if the crate contains archive materials from the burned library. Same skeleton, completely different flavour, zero extra prep.

### At the Table, Right Now

When the party approaches the Guild board, you don't describe individual postings. You say:

_"The board has maybe thirty notices on it — a mix of city work orders, private contracts, and a few with no name attached at all. A few catch your eye."_

Then you offer three. Pick three skeletons, dress them with nouns from your sheet. The party picks one or asks about others. If they ask about others, you dress another skeleton on the spot. You never run out because the skeletons don't run out.
